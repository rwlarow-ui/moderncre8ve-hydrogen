## Executive Summary

ModernCre8ve is a handcrafted modern furniture brand rooted in mid-century, Scandinavian, and Japandi design sensibilities. The brand was founded by a Vermont-raised designer who brought old-world making values to a new-world aesthetic — first in New York, then Los Angeles — and eventually built a model where intentional design leadership is paired with the generational craftsmanship of Amish woodworkers in the Midwest.

The brand voice is intentional, warm, and quietly confident. It doesn't shout. It doesn't chase trends. It speaks to people who believe their home is an expression of who they are — and who are willing to wait 12–16 weeks for something worth keeping. The tone is personal and narrative-led on brand story pages, educational and expectation-setting in transactional contexts, and emotionally resonant in marketing. Every word should reinforce the idea that choosing ModernCre8ve is a considered, values-driven act — not an impulse purchase.

The brand's COVID-era pivot is a core part of its identity: scaling down the in-house workshop to focus entirely on design and the customer relationship, and partnering with Amish craftsmen who share the same obsession with craft, is not a story of compromise — it's a story of conviction. That narrative thread runs through everything.

## We Are / We Are Not

The foundational brand identity anchor. Voice is constant — it doesn't change by channel or audience.

| We Are | We Are Not |
|--------|------------|
| **Intentional** — every word, every design decision is deliberate | **Impulsive** — not trend-chasing or reactive to what's popular |
| **Warm & Personal** — we speak like a founder who genuinely cares, not a brand that performs caring | **Corporate or distant** — no stiff formality, no generic marketing-speak |
| **Confident without arrogance** — we believe deeply in our work but don't talk down to customers | **Boastful or precious** — no "luxury" posturing or exclusivity language |
| **Grounded in craft** — we name the wood, the maker, the process; specificity is a form of respect | **Vague or aspirational-hollow** — no "quality you can feel" fluff without substance |
| **Patient and honest** — we set real expectations about lead times and limitations upfront | **Evasive or over-promising** — we don't hide the 12–16 week wait or imply broader customization than exists |
| **Values-led** — we talk about where and how things are made because it matters to us and our customers | **Greenwashing or performative** — we don't overclaim sustainability; we're honest about what we do |

### Voice Attributes Detail

#### Intentional
- **What it means**: Design decisions, material choices, and words are never accidental. Every product and every piece of copy reflects deliberate thinking.
- **How it shows up**: Use specific, considered language. Name the wood species. Describe the "why" behind design choices. Avoid filler phrases.
- **What to avoid**: Generic superlatives ("amazing quality"), vague adjectives ("beautiful craftsmanship" without showing it), trend-reactive copy.
- **Evidence**: "Every aspect of our furniture is carefully considered, intentional, meticulously crafted from original thought." (070323 meeting – Home/About/Contact pages)
- **Confidence**: High

#### Warm & Personal
- **What it means**: The brand has a human face — the founder's story is central. Copy feels like it comes from a person, not a committee.
- **How it shows up**: First-person brand narrative (founder voice), "from our family to yours" framing, emotionally resonant language about home and connection.
- **What to avoid**: Brand-voice-by-committee blandness. Don't sand down the personal edges to sound "professional."
- **Evidence**: "I grew up in Stowe, Vermont, in a family of woodworkers and maple syrup makers..." (MC8 About page 0623); "from our family to yours" (meeting notes)
- **Confidence**: High

#### Confident without Arrogance
- **What it means**: We believe deeply in what we make and aren't afraid to say it — but we're never condescending, never exclusionary, never "luxury brand" posturing.
- **How it shows up**: Declarative statements about craft and design conviction. No hedging, but also no gatekeeping. Accessible to someone buying their first real piece of furniture.
- **What to avoid**: "Luxury," "exclusive," "discerning," "elevated" — these words create distance. Also avoid false modesty.
- **Evidence**: "A Passion for Excellence: We deliver real-wood furniture that is expertly crafted, durable, and built for longevity." (meeting notes); customer-facing tone throughout is aspirational but never elitist.
- **Confidence**: High

#### Grounded in Craft
- **What it means**: Specificity signals credibility. We don't say "quality wood" — we say "quartersawn white oak." We don't say "made with care" — we say "old-world Amish craftsmen in the Midwest."
- **How it shows up**: Name materials by species. Explain production partnership. Describe process details. Make the invisible visible.
- **What to avoid**: Generic craft language ("handmade with love," "artisanal quality") that every DTC furniture brand uses. Specificity differentiates.
- **Evidence**: Approved wood list in brand docs: walnut, hard maple, cherry, white oak, quartersawn white oak. Production origin: Amish craftsmen, Midwest USA. (meeting notes, onboarding doc)
- **Confidence**: High

#### Patient and Honest
- **What it means**: We set real expectations and don't hide uncomfortable truths (long lead times, limited customization). Honesty is a form of respect for the customer.
- **How it shows up**: Lead times stated clearly and early. Customization limitations framed as curation, not restriction. No bait-and-switch language.
- **What to avoid**: Burying lead time information. Implying customization options that don't exist. Vague promises about delivery.
- **Evidence**: "Ok to lead with [customization] but limited choices. Avoid stain matching etc. 2 choices only + natural." (onboarding doc); lead time 12–16 weeks consistent across all sources.
- **Confidence**: High

#### Values-Led
- **What it means**: We care about where and how things are made — sustainable domestic hardwoods, Amish partnership, charitable giving — and we say so honestly, without overclaiming.
- **How it shows up**: Mention FSC-certified domestic hardwoods. Reference Amish partnership as a values alignment, not just a supply chain fact. Acknowledge charitable giving (Water.org, Save the Children, Direct Relief) without making it the centerpiece.
- **What to avoid**: Greenwashing language ("eco-friendly," "sustainable" without specifics). Don't make charitable giving sound transactional.
- **Evidence**: "Gather your loved ones around a table that's a conversation piece in itself — consciously-made, supporting the growth and well-being of a community of local artisans, and preserving the environment for future generations." (meeting notes)
- **Confidence**: Medium — charitable giving amounts/percentages not confirmed in docs

- **vs. West Elm / Crate & Barrel**: We're not mass-produced or trend-dependent. Our pieces are handcrafted to order; you're not buying inventory off a warehouse floor.
- **vs. RoveConcepts / Article**: We use domestic solid hardwoods (not veneers or composites) and Amish craftsmanship with multi-generational expertise. Not just "mid-century inspired" — genuinely built to last.
- **vs. Design Within Reach / DWR**: Similar aesthetic conviction, but made in the Midwest by craftsmen, not imported. And significantly more accessible in price for the quality tier.
- **vs. Status Quo (fast furniture)**: "No more throw-away furniture that ends up on the curb." Investment pieces that can be passed down.

### Context-Specific Guidelines

#### About / Brand Story Page
- **Overall tone**: Personal, narrative, founder-voiced
- **Opening approach**: Start with the human story (Vermont, woodworking family, the pivot) before the product
- **Do's**: Use "I" for founder voice; be specific about place names, wood types, partner relationships; let the COVID pivot story breathe
- **Don'ts**: Don't open with product specs or awards; don't sound like a press release; avoid passive voice
- **Example**: "I grew up in Stowe, Vermont, in a family of woodworkers and maple syrup makers..."

#### Product Pages
- **Overall tone**: Warm but precise; emotionally evocative + technically credible
- **Opening approach**: Lead with what the piece *does* for a space, then substantiate with craft details
- **Do's**: Name the wood species; mention Amish craftsmanship; state lead time clearly; describe the tactile and visual experience
- **Don'ts**: Don't hide lead time; don't imply customization beyond approved options; don't use generic adjectives like "premium" without evidence
- **Example**: "Handcrafted to order in quartersawn white oak by our Amish partners in the Midwest. Expected delivery: 12–16 weeks."

#### Email Marketing
- **Overall tone**: Warm, personal, one-to-one — not broadcast
- **Opening approach**: Add value before selling; use storytelling, process content, or customer stories to open
- **Do's**: Reference the pillars contextually (a Craft-focused send, a Beauty-focused send, a Care-focused send); include personal touches from the founder
- **Don'ts**: Don't lead with discount; don't use urgency tactics that conflict with the "patient" brand voice; avoid stiff subject line formulas
- **Example subject line**: "This is what 400 hours of craftsmanship looks like" / "The table that started it all"

#### Social Media (Instagram / Pinterest)
- **Overall tone**: Visually-led; words amplify the image, not describe it
- **Caption approach**: Open with an evocative line, not a product name. Let the visual do the heavy lifting.
- **Do's**: Short captions; reference design philosophy; use customer language (beautiful, exquisite, love); tell micro-stories from production
- **Don'ts**: Don't include product specs in captions; don't use hashtag-stuffing; avoid "Shop now" as the only CTA
- **Example**: "Some things take time. ✦ Quartersawn white oak, Midwest-made, yours in 14 weeks."

#### Customer Service
- **Overall tone**: Honest, warm, unhurried
- **Lead time conversations**: Frame the wait positively — "Your piece is being handcrafted for you specifically; that takes time." Never apologize for the process.
- **Customization conversations**: Be direct about what's available. Don't leave ambiguity. "We offer 5 wood species — walnut, maple, cherry, white oak, and quartersawn white oak. No stain matching, but the natural variation in each species gives every piece its own character."
- **Do's**: Acknowledge the customer's emotion first; be specific; offer what you *can* do
- **Don'ts**: Don't over-promise; don't use corporate escalation language; don't deflect with policy

## Language to Avoid

### Anti-Patterns

1. **"Premium quality furniture"** — Problem: Hollow; every furniture brand says this. Better: Name what makes it premium — "quartersawn white oak, Amish-crafted, built to outlast the trends."

2. **"Shop our collection"** as the only CTA — Problem: Transactional, not brand-building. Better: "Find your piece" / "Start your order" / "See what's possible in [wood species]"

3. **"Fast, free shipping"** — Problem: Factually wrong and brand-damaging. Better: "Handcrafted to order — delivered with white glove installation, typically in 12–16 weeks."

4. **Burying lead time** — Problem: Creates frustration and returns. Better: State it early, frame it as a feature. "Your piece is made for you. That takes 12–16 weeks — and it's worth every day."

5. **"We offer fully customizable furniture"** — Problem: Sets expectations we can't meet. Better: "Choose from 5 domestic hardwoods — each species with its own character and grain pattern."

6. **Flowery, abstract copy** — Problem: Pre-2023 brand voice; too vague to be credible. Better: Be specific. Trade "immerse yourself in the exquisite tactile sensation" for "run your hand across the grain of quartersawn white oak."
